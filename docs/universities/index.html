Why CalmCampus

Many students struggle quietly.

Most universities already offer good support. The hard part is that students are busy, stigma is real and it is not always clear where to start. We want to see whether a very light check-in fits into that.

Light enough to keep

Four yes or no questions, once a week, in under a minute. Every question can be skipped.

Alongside student services

We do not want to replace counsellors or student services. We would like to learn how a small tool can sit next to them.

Private by design

Students are more likely to be honest when they know nobody else can see their answers.

Privacy

Answers stay on the phone.

Privacy is how the app is built, not a line in a policy. What a student shares with CalmCampus is for them, and it never becomes something a university can look up.

On-device only

Check-in answers are stored on the student's phone. There is no server holding them.

No analytics

No trackers and no ad networks. Nothing about an individual student reaches the university.

No accounts

No name, email or login is needed to use the app.

Wellbeing, not diagnosis

CalmCampus does not diagnose or treat anything, and it does not replace professional care.

Research

Built on research. Designed to be tested.

We reviewed 35 studies and reports. The need is well documented. What is still open is whether CalmCampus itself helps anyone. That is something we would like to test.

The need, in published studies

1 in 3

KU Leuven freshmen (n = 4,921) reported a mental health problem in the past year. These problems went with a lower academic year percentage.

Bruffaerts et al., J. Affective Disorders, 2018
35%

of 13,984 first-year students in 8 countries, Belgium included, screened positive for a common mental health condition at some point in their life (31% in the past year).

Auerbach et al., J. Abnormal Psychology, 2018
23%

of 27,446 Dutch students in the 2025 national monitor had above-average positive mental health ("flourishing"). Five of ten wellbeing measures had improved since 2021.

Nuijen et al., RIVM and Trimbos, 2025

Why privacy and a short check-in

24.6%

of first-years said they would definitely seek help for an emotional problem. The most common reason not to: wanting to handle it alone (56.4%).

Ebert et al., Int. J. Methods in Psychiatric Research, 2019
Small effects

Digital self-help helps students a little: g = 0.18 for depression and 0.20 for stress in 48 trials. We take that as a reason to measure carefully.

Harrer et al., Int. J. Methods in Psychiatric Research, 2019

Where the evidence is thin

0.82 to 0.57

A phone-based depression model looked accurate in 57 students (AUC 0.82) and was close to chance in a mixed sample of 5,262 (AUC 0.57). That is why CalmCampus does not read phone data and asks students directly instead.

Müller et al., Scientific Reports, 2021
33 of 36

top-ranked depression and smoking-cessation apps sent data to third parties, often without saying so. Keeping data on the phone avoids this one risk.

Huckvale et al., JAMA Network Open, 2019
3.3%

of mental health app users were still active after 30 days in real-world data. Keeping students engaged is our biggest challenge, and we would like to measure it.

Baumel et al., J. Medical Internet Research, 2019

What we do not claim

  • CalmCampus is a wellness tool, not a medical device. It does not diagnose or treat, and it does not replace student services or professional care.
  • No study has tested CalmCampus yet. The studies above tested other programmes and support the reasoning behind the design, not the product.
  • We do not claim the app can spot burnout or any condition. It asks a few questions and nothing more.
  • Where digital self-help works, effects in students are small, and we do not claim more.

What we would like to test

Questions only. There are no results yet, nothing is scheduled, and any study would be designed with researchers and ethics advisers.

  • Engagement. Do students keep a weekly check-in going across a semester?
  • Trust. Do students understand that their answers stay on the phone, and does it matter to them?
  • Wellbeing. What changes over a semester, compared with a comparison group?
  • Reaching support. Does the app make it easier or harder for students to reach student services? We do not know yet.
  • Safety. How often does a check-in make someone feel worse, and how should that be handled?

We intend to share what we find openly, including results that do not favour us.

Working with universities

We would like to start with a conversation.

CalmCampus began at the KU Leuven KICK Challenge 2026. That is where the idea started, and it is not an endorsement by the university. We are talking with universities in Belgium and the Netherlands, and we would like to test the app with student services and students.

What we would like to do together

  • Hear what student services already do, and where a light check-in could fit or not fit.
  • Agree on research questions worth testing, and how to test them responsibly.
  • Look at ethics, privacy and data protection together before anything involves students.

Who we would like to talk to

  • Student services and counsellors who can help us do this responsibly.
  • Researchers in student mental health, ethics and privacy-preserving technology.
  • Students who would like to try the app when it is ready and tell us honestly what works.

Nothing is scheduled and no institution has agreed to anything. The ask is small: a 20-minute call, to see whether there is something worth doing.
